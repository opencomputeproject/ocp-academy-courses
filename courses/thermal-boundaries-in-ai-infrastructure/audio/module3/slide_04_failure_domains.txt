Component reliability and system availability describe different outcomes. The distinction becomes especially important when cooling infrastructure is shared.

At the paper's representative cold-plate design point, lower junction temperature reduces modeled processor failures enough to outweigh the added hydraulic components. But as plates and connectors spread across more components, the model's plumbing terms grow. The initial component-reliability benefit can erode or invert under an extended configuration and different assumed rates.

A shared coolant distribution unit creates another kind of exposure. A fault in an individual component may affect a small part of the system. A shared control, exchanger, valve, or other common path can affect the entire cooling domain that depends on it. Pump redundancy can address particular pump failures without proving that all shared modes have been removed.

Immersion also has shared infrastructure, such as a pump and filtration skid serving a tank or row. Its environmental benefits and smaller coupling count do not eliminate the need to examine that failure domain.

The diagram is a generic dependency example. It does not claim a measured failure rate for Deschutes, Brazos, or any other product. The paper's rack-level outage assumptions and its component failure counts should not be applied interchangeably.

For a real design, ask what fails, how much hardware loses cooling, how isolation works, what remains available during service, and what the software must tolerate. Then obtain evidence for the actual configuration.
