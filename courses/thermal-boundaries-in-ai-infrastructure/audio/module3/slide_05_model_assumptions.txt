The cost bars are results of a declared case. They are not observations of three complete facilities, and they are not a universal ranking of cooling technologies.

The paper compares hypothetical, fully saturated air, single-phase cold-plate, and single-phase immersion deployments within a two-hundred-megawatt facility envelope. I T pricing is normalized to remove asserted server-price differences from the comparison. Energy price, lifecycle treatment, repair costs, and reliability inputs are declared so readers can examine the consequences of cooling choices under that construction.

Real facilities commonly mix technologies, expand in phases, and operate under different climates, prices, workloads, service arrangements, and availability requirements. Those differences need their own analysis. The comparison also has explicit exclusions, including no hybrid case or fluid-replenishment cost line.

The reliability evidence has more than one category. A conservative immersion model gives an improvement under its assumed mechanism shares. A reported fleet observation supplies a different kind of evidence and a higher reported ceiling. The latter is not a guarantee for hardware and operating conditions that were not part of that observation.

Likewise, cold-plate results differ between the initial design point and a more extensively plumbed stress case. Repair cost assumptions can strongly affect the economic result, especially when a failure is priced as a share of expensive server value.

Use the bars to ask a sensitivity question: which input could change this deployment's conclusion? Substitute relevant evidence, keep the service objective consistent, and preserve the distinction between an assumed rate and a measured one. That makes a model useful for a decision without claiming it has already validated the site.
