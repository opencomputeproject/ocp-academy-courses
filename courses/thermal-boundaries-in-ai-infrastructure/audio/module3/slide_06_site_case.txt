Apply the framework to a fictional upgrade. The processor's cold plate holds its junction condition. The surrounding memory and networking load is growing, and the site has limited power and space. The proposal is to add more cooling coverage while keeping deployment disruption manageable.

Begin with the demonstrated constraint. The processor acquisition path is not the current failure. The distributed remainder needs characterization, and the facility's available power and footprint constrain the answer. Adding plates, improving the volume path, or qualifying a complementary arrangement are hypotheses to evaluate, not automatic recommendations.

For each candidate, trace the new dependencies. Which hardware or material qualification changes? What service access is required? Which interface crosses into the facility? Which controls and telemetry tell the operator whether the combined system is working?

The economic case must include the cost of extending the incumbent and the cost of preparing the alternative. Supply-chain readiness matters because a thermally attractive option is not deployable at the required scale if its qualified components, service capability, or acceptance process are unavailable.

Open, agreed interfaces can reduce the burden of coordinating those changes. They also need actual compatibility evidence. A familiar connector name or a matching nominal rating is not proof of interoperability for the installed arrangement.

Brazos examines a specific liquid-to-air option and its site conditions. Open Data Center for A I examines facility compatibility and capacity targets. Use those courses when the framework has identified the implementation or facility question that needs detail.

The review result should name the candidate, the evidence needed, the accountable organizations, and the unresolved decisions. That is a concrete readiness plan even before an architecture is selected.
