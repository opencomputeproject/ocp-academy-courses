Cooling has three jobs. Acquisition takes heat from the component. Transport carries the captured heat across the server and rack boundary. Rejection transfers it to the outside environment or a heat-reuse system.

Heat flux governs the hardest local acquisition task. Total heat sets what transport and rejection must carry. Adequate capacity at one step does not establish adequate capacity at the others.

A rear-door heat exchanger illustrates the distinction. It accepts heat that chassis air has already acquired from the electronics. It can improve the downstream path, but does not change the ability of the air-cooled surface to hold a particular processor's junction temperature. If acquisition fails first, more downstream rejection alone cannot restore that margin.

The animation then follows a different case: a cold plate solves the processor hotspot, while the distributed remainder still needs its own path. That remaining load must be accounted for through all three jobs. It cannot disappear from the system heat balance simply because the main chip moved to liquid.

The ownership boundary matters too. Acquisition is tied closely to the I T design cycle. Facility rejection changes on a much longer building lifecycle. Transport is the shared seam where their requirements must meet.

Deschutes illustrates a liquid-to-liquid interface to facility water. Brazos illustrates a liquid-to-air interface to an air-cooled site. Their detailed operating conditions belong in their courses. Here, use them to ask which job and which owner a proposal changes, and which upstream duties remain.
