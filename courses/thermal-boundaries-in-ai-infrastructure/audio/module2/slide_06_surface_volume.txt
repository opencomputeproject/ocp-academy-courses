Compare cooling methods by the thermal job they cover, rather than arranging them as a single ladder with one winner at the top.

A cold plate is a surface method. It targets a particular component and can be engineered for concentrated heat. A single-phase plate transports that heat by warming its coolant. A two-phase plate uses phase change to extend the flux and temperature-uniformity opportunity, while introducing pressure, vapor-management, wetting, and dry-out considerations.

The paper describes flow boiling as its representative two-phase cold-plate regime. The dedicated Two-Phase D L C course also discusses pool-boiling cold-plate designs. Those are different implementations. Do not turn one representative description into the definition of every two-phase cold plate.

Immersion is a volume method. A dielectric fluid contacts the wider system, covering the distributed load together with the hotspots it can acquire adequately. Single-phase immersion gains broad reach, but its fluid properties and flow design still constrain the most demanding local surfaces. Precision flow or a complementary surface method can be relevant there.

Two-phase immersion uses boiling in the bath and condensation to return liquid. Its thermal opportunity is accompanied by working-fluid, containment, compatibility, and service requirements. Those dependencies can determine whether a promising thermal arrangement is appropriate for a deployment.

Material and high-speed electrical qualification matter for immersion because hardware validated in air is not automatically validated in a fluid environment. The dielectric label does not settle every compatibility question.

The useful comparison has two parts: which heat does the method cover, and what new dependency must be accepted to obtain that coverage?
