The move to liquid began at the concentrated hotspot. A cold plate can remove heat from a well-defined processor surface, where high local flux defeats a practical air-cooled arrangement. But that leaves a second thermal problem.

Memory, network silicon, optics, storage, and power-conversion losses are distributed through the system. Each load may be modest compared with the main processor, yet the combined remainder can be substantial. Its geometry is less convenient, and components can obstruct one another's airflow.

The paper's distributed-load figure is a model tied to representative platform configurations. It separates the non-processor load from the hottest processor and nearby concentrated memory load. Published anchors, announced designs, and extrapolation have different evidence bases. The curve should therefore be read as a changing coverage problem, not a guaranteed forecast for every rack.

Once cold plates handle the processors, the remaining air path still needs to carry the distributed heat. Increasing capture at one hotspot does not necessarily solve that remainder. Adding plates component by component may improve coverage, but it also adds interfaces and the complexity debt discussed in module one.

A volume approach instead exposes the broader system to its cooling medium. Air already does this; immersion applies the whole-system idea using a denser dielectric medium. Either arrangement still needs appropriate acquisition at its most demanding locations.

For the next architecture decision, ask what the cold plates capture, what they leave behind, and how the remainder is distributed. The binding constraint can move after an upgrade, even when the upgraded processor is comfortable.
