The approximately four-kilowatt discussion is a reference architectural boundary, not a universal limit on single-phase cooling. Appendix B makes that qualification explicit.

Its comparison uses water at a thirty-five-degree inlet, five liters per minute, a thirty-millimeter flow length, and a ten-degree permitted coolant rise. Under those flow and temperature-rise conditions, sensible heat transport is approximately three point four six kilowatts. Changing the conditions changes that calorimetric result.

The finer channel geometries can increase local heat-transfer capability, but the hydraulic penalty rises sharply. The very fine reference geometry approaches four kilowatts of nominal modeled heat removal while requiring roughly seventeen bar of pressure drop and exceeding the ten-degree coolant-rise budget. A promising heat-transfer number therefore does not demonstrate a feasible complete system.

Pressure also creates mechanical loading. Deflection can worsen package contact and thermal resistance, so the hydraulic and thermal analyses cannot be separated from the mechanical design. Increasing flow may recover coolant-rise margin while increasing pressure and mechanical demands.

The paper also compares a skived-fin approach that gains wetted area without relying on the most extreme velocity. That example reinforces the design lesson: maximizing a local heat-transfer coefficient is not the same as optimizing the whole cold plate.

A different geometry, parallel path, coolant condition, or operating allowance may extend the envelope. Each change needs evidence against the actual constraints.
