A modern system can contain different thermal problems at the same time. The toolbox approach matches methods to those problems instead of requiring one method to solve everything.

Air remains useful for suitable moderate loads and for hardware whose operational simplicity matters. A surface method can take the most concentrated hotspot. A qualified volume method can address the distributed surroundings. The handoff between them must be engineered and verified, because combining two capable parts does not automatically create a capable system.

Microfluidics introduces another lever. Channels within or beneath the silicon can reduce the resistance between the heat source and the coolant. The recovered temperature budget can support more power or warmer coolant conditions. It still needs a downstream route to transport and reject the heat. The paper therefore presents microfluidics as an emerging extension of other methods, rather than a complete facility cooling architecture on its own.

Keep maturity visible. A promising research or co-design direction is not equivalent to a qualified production option for the hardware being purchased today. Its interfaces, manufacturing path, and operating evidence need their own assessment.

The same care applies to the facility. A changed technology cooling system and coolant distribution unit may preserve a familiar facility-water path if their temperatures, flows, and interfaces remain compatible. A boiling-fluid change does not by itself prove that all upstream infrastructure must be replaced.

Open Rack Module seven provides the mechanical detail of manifolds and couplings. Here, decide what combination earns its dependencies, then require evidence at each handoff. That is how the toolbox becomes an architecture instead of a list of technologies.
