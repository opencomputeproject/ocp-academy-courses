A large heat-rejection plant does not prove that a processor can be cooled. The first task is to identify where the thermal path is failing.

Total package power tells us how much heat must eventually leave the system. Heat flux tells us how concentrated that heat is at the surface where it must be captured. The same total power spread across a larger area presents a different acquisition problem from power concentrated into a smaller hotspot.

The junction-temperature budget matters too. Between the silicon and the coolant sits a chain of thermal resistances. As heat passing through that chain increases, each resistance consumes more of the available temperature difference. An interface imperfection that was tolerable at a lower load can become decisive at a higher flux. Power alone therefore does not tell us which device reaches its limit first.

For a single-phase fluid, the heat carried depends on mass flow, specific heat capacity, and the permitted temperature rise. Increasing flow can carry more heat, but hydraulic resistance and the energy needed to move the fluid make that an increasingly costly response. Allowing a larger temperature rise is also constrained by device and coolant conditions.

Consider a proposed upgrade where the room plant has spare capacity but one component exceeds its junction limit. The open question is the local acquisition path, not simply the building's total heat-removal rating. Start there before selecting a larger downstream system.
