The most revealing part of the framework is the gap between economic disadvantage and physical infeasibility. The paper calls it the avoidance zone.

Inside that zone, the incumbent still works. It can be extended to defer a disruptive change, but doing so carries a rising cost. That cost may appear as more fan power, larger heatsinks, more rack space, additional plates and connectors, or more demanding service. The exact form depends on the method and the deployment.

A narrow avoidance zone leaves little room to defer the transition. Once the economics deteriorate, the physical limit follows relatively soon. A wide zone creates a longer opportunity to keep extending the incumbent, even when the full business case favors another arrangement.

The animation is conceptual. Its operating point moves from a comfortable range into the avoidance zone and toward the feasibility boundary. The axes do not establish a universal device-power threshold or a required switch date. They show the relationship between continuing to extend the current method and accepting the cost of changing it.

This explains why a method's ability to work is an incomplete decision rule. A proposal can be thermally achievable and still use scarce power, space, labor, or reliability margin badly. Conversely, the replacement may require readiness that the site does not yet have.

Use the diagram to make both sides visible. What does extending the incumbent cost? What does switching require? Which constraints could end the available runway? The useful decision is about the whole transition, not only whether today's temperature remains acceptable.
