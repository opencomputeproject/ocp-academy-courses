An attractive business case does not automatically give anyone the authority to change the architecture. The paper's least-disruption law describes an ecosystem that exhausts options preserving the current arrangement before accepting a new dependency.

Those dependencies are concrete. A cooling change may require different server interfaces, rack plumbing, facility connections, qualified suppliers, and service procedures. The organization initiating the change may not control the parties that must absorb it. Each participant therefore has a reason to avoid disruption while the incumbent can still be made to work.

The paper names an agency mismatch. The operator experiences energy bills, failures, and service complexity. The silicon supplier controls important design decisions and faces the point where the product cannot perform within its thermal envelope. The party carrying the operating cost is not necessarily the party able to change the design.

Vertically integrated organizations can align those roles. If one organization designs the silicon and operates the site, it may act on the economic signal earlier. That is an exception within the framework, not evidence that every operator follows one identical adoption clock.

The least-disruption law is the authors' explanation of adoption behavior. It is not a thermodynamic law, a command to wait, or an exact forecast for a particular technology.

For a review, name the required changes and the organizations that can authorize them. Then distinguish a technical obstacle from a coordination obstacle. A transition becomes easier to plan when the cost, design authority, and readiness obligations are visible together.
