Two kinds of debt explain different costs of delaying a transition. Keeping them separate helps identify an action that can actually reduce the problem.

Preparation debt is unbuilt ecosystem readiness. The alternative may need qualified components, supplier capacity, interoperable interfaces, trained staff, and a validated commissioning approach. If those capabilities are deferred while the incumbent still works, they are missing when a forced transition creates sudden demand. No single organization necessarily owns the whole bill.

Complexity debt accumulates while the incumbent is extended. For example, a surface method can acquire more distributed heat by adding plates to more components. Each addition also brings interfaces, connections, qualification, and service obligations. The architecture gains coverage, but its operational burden can grow faster than the initial design argument suggests.

Adding another plate might solve today's local temperature problem. It does not automatically reduce preparation debt for a different future architecture. Likewise, qualifying a second supplier prepares an option, but does not remove the plumbing already installed in the incumbent.

The Deschutes course provides a concrete example of supply-chain preparation through its qualified supplier model. That is a useful follow-on study of readiness, rather than a supplier table to reproduce here.

The practical question is which debt a proposed action addresses. Prepare alternative interfaces and capabilities before they become urgent. At the same time, keep the cost of extending the current method visible. Readiness can be chosen even when the precise date of the next transition cannot.
