A cooling method can become a poor choice before it becomes incapable of doing the job. The three boundaries help distinguish those situations.

The efficiency boundary is the point where cooling overhead starts to grow disproportionately. It is the knee of a curve, not a universal percentage. The method still holds the device within specification, but each additional increment of useful compute demands a growing share of power for fans or pumps.

The economic boundary asks a wider question. Count capital, energy, maintenance, repair consequences, service labor, and the space the architecture consumes. A method can remain thermally feasible while another arrangement provides a better total-cost outcome. Crossing this boundary means the incumbent's full economics have turned against it under the comparison being made.

The physics boundary is different. Within the available geometry, flow, pressure, temperatures, and device rating, the method cannot maintain the required junction condition. Spending more on the same operating arrangement no longer solves the constraint. The options include reducing the compute load or changing the architecture.

Imagine a system that still holds temperature, but now requires expensive hardware additions and service effort. That evidence points toward the economic boundary. If achievable operating conditions cannot hold the junction temperature at the required workload, it points toward the physics boundary.

The response depends on the classification. An energy penalty calls for efficiency analysis. A cost disadvantage calls for an architectural business case. A feasibility failure requires a change in operating demand or design.
