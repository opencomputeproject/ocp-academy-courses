# pronunciation: 1,500 volts DC -> "fifteen hundred volts D C"; 400 volts DC -> "four hundred volts D C"; Mt. Diablo -> "Mount Diablo"; 800 VDC -> "eight hundred volts D C"; 48-volt -> "forty-eight-volt"; LVDC -> "L V D C"; OCP -> "O C P"; IT -> "I T"

For the same transferred power, increasing voltage reduces current. Power equals voltage times current. Resistive conductor loss depends on current squared times resistance, so reducing current can reduce losses in a particular conductor path.

That relationship is driving a move from familiar forty-eight-volt rack buses toward higher-voltage direct current, including eight hundred volts D C. Eight hundred volts remains within low-voltage direct current, or L V D C. O C P's L V D C scope extends up to fifteen hundred volts D C. Low voltage names the class; insulation, protection and service requirements still matter.

The published Mount Diablo sidecar illustrates a transition path. It moves power conversion beside the I T rack and supplies plus and minus four hundred volts D C, or eight hundred volts between poles. Moving toward native eight hundred volts D C facility distribution extends the change upstream. These architectures still need agreement on earthing, conversion, protection and equipment compatibility.

Moving conversion can free space in the I T rack and change where heat and maintenance work occur. Judge efficiency across the complete power path, rather than assuming that a higher voltage or fewer visible boxes guarantees a better system.
