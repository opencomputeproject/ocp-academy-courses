Wide-bandgap semiconductors make high-voltage, high-frequency switching more practical. The specification highlights silicon carbide and notes emerging gallium nitride potential. Faster switching can shrink the magnetic components needed for isolation because transformer size is strongly related to operating frequency. That creates the power-density opportunity behind the SST. The trade is not free. Faster edges increase electromagnetic-interference challenges, insulation stress, common-mode behavior, and control complexity. High-frequency magnetics must manage core loss, winding loss, thermal gradients, partial discharge, and manufacturability at megawatt scale. The technology decision is therefore a system optimization, not a single device comparison.

Silicon carbide devices generally offer lower switching loss and higher temperature capability than traditional silicon devices at relevant voltage classes, but packaging, gate drive, short-circuit withstand, and cooling remain decisive. Gallium nitride can switch very quickly, yet the product voltage and power level determine whether it is a practical fit today. Magnetics face a similar maturity question. Raising frequency reduces required core area, while skin effect, proximity effect, core loss, insulation stress, and parasitic capacitance can grow. The winning design balances device, package, magnetic, cooling, and control behavior over the full load range—not at one laboratory operating point.
