An SST can actively regulate output, shape input current, report detailed telemetry, coordinate with storage, and potentially support bidirectional functions when a product is designed for them. Modularity may improve serviceability and graceful degradation. Those benefits are design targets, not automatic outcomes. Semiconductor count, gate-drive complexity, cooling, firmware validation, fault containment, component availability, and certification all scale with product ambition. At five or ten megawatts, a small failure-rate assumption becomes operationally significant. The right review question is not whether SSTs are better in the abstract. It is whether the validated product meets the required efficiency, availability, protection, and maintainability envelope for a defined site architecture.

Modularity needs definition. It can mean series-connected power cells for voltage, parallel modules for current, field-replaceable subassemblies, or redundant control and cooling paths. Each form solves a different problem. A modular topology may isolate a failed cell, but only if voltage sharing, bypass hardware, controls, and thermal behavior remain safe. Bidirectional operation likewise should not be assumed merely because power electronics are present. It requires intentional device topology, controls, protection, interconnection approval, and an application that values reverse power flow. In a product review, convert appealing capability words into a testable operating mode, limit, and failure response.
