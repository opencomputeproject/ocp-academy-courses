The conventional path separates two major functions. A bulky fifty- or sixty-hertz transformer steps medium voltage down, and one or more rectifier units convert that lower-voltage AC to DC. Regulation and grid-support behavior are distributed across additional equipment. An SST integrates power-electronic conversion, high-frequency isolation, regulation, sensing, and control into a coordinated product. The important distinction is not that one box replaces every facility function. It is that isolation and voltage conversion move into a switched power architecture. This can reduce stages and unlock active control, but it also concentrates semiconductor, cooling, protection, and firmware responsibilities inside the product.

Line-frequency equipment has advantages as well as limitations. It is mature, widely understood, and supported by established protection and maintenance practices. Its magnetic components are large, but the energy path can be comparatively simple and passive. The SST replaces some of that passive behavior with controllable electronics. It can shape current and respond rapidly, yet it must keep operating through switching-device faults, sensor errors, control-software anomalies, cooling problems, and auxiliary-power disturbances. Evaluation should therefore compare complete life-cycle performance—losses at load, footprint, maintainability, availability, fault behavior, spares, and certification—not only nameplate efficiency or enclosure size.
