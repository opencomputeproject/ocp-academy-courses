SemiAnalysis describes the transition in four phases. First, high-voltage DC power appears in a retrofit sidecar near the rack. Second, more compute equipment becomes native to the higher-voltage DC bus. Third, rectification moves to a centralized facility location, eliminating repeated row-level conversion. Fourth, a solid state transformer moves the conversion boundary to medium voltage and produces the facility DC bus directly. This is a useful architecture lens because each phase moves a boundary, not merely a component. It is still an analyst roadmap. OCP specifications define interoperable products and interfaces; they do not guarantee the dates or adoption sequence shown in market analysis.

Phase boundaries also help explain why the rack sidecar remains relevant. A sidecar can introduce high-voltage DC distribution without rebuilding the medium-voltage entrance or replacing every facility power train. Native eight-hundred-volt compute then reduces repeated rack conversion. Central rectification consolidates equipment in gray space. The SST phase goes further by integrating medium-voltage conversion and isolation. None of these moves eliminates the need for rack-level regulation; processors still need low-voltage, high-current rails. The key change is where the large power steps occur and which layer owns buffering, backup, telemetry, and protection.
