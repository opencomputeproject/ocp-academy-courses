The key physics begins with power equal to voltage times current. For a one-megawatt idealized load, fifty-four volts would imply about eighteen and a half kiloamps, while eight hundred volts implies one point two five kiloamps. That is roughly a fifteen-to-one current ratio before allowing for conversion efficiency, redundancy, or design margins. Resistive loss is current squared times resistance, so reducing current has an outsized effect on conductor heating. The animation uses a fixed resistance only to expose the relationship; it is not a cable-sizing calculation. Real designs also consider insulation, creepage, protection, connectors, allowable temperature rise, and fault energy. Higher voltage does not make those obligations disappear. It changes the engineering trade.

The comparison also clarifies why conductor resistance matters twice: it produces direct electrical loss and indirect cooling demand. If resistance stays fixed, dividing current by roughly fourteen point eight divides I-squared-R loss by roughly two hundred nineteen. In practice, engineers use some of that advantage to reduce conductor cross-section, so the full theoretical loss reduction is not realized. The system still benefits through a different balance of copper mass, cable count, voltage drop, routing, and heat. Conversely, eight hundred volts increases arc, insulation, clearance, connector, touch-safety, and disconnect requirements. A sound architecture spends the current advantage deliberately instead of assuming the voltage change solves the entire delivery problem.
