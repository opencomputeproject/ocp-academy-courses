Appendix A shows five application patterns: a catcher or block-redundant arrangement, distributed-redundant busway, N plus C busway, N plus C switchboard, and N-redundant busway integration. The figures are non-normative. Their value is in the questions they expose. Where is spare capacity held? Which busway or switchboard connects it? What isolation is required after a fault? Can one SST, feeder, or distribution section be maintained without exceeding another component’s rating? The animation shows a generic parallel architecture transferring load away from a faulted block. Real coordination depends on protection timing, available current, bus segmentation, and the load’s ride-through capability.

In a catcher system, shared spare capacity may support a failed block. Distributed redundancy can spread capacity across busways. N plus C expresses the capacity needed for load plus additional converter blocks, while switchboard and busway variants place isolation and distribution boundaries differently. N-redundant integration can allocate independent paths to loads. For each pattern, calculate normal and contingency loading, fault current, transfer or control timing, common-mode failure exposure, maintenance isolation, and recovery. A drawing that shows an extra block is not yet an availability result. The spare must be electrically reachable and operationally ready when the failure occurs.
