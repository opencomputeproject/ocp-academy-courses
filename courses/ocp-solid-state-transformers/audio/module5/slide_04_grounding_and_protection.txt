The SST output may be floating or high-resistance grounded, with final detail still TBD. That choice changes how the first ground fault is detected, what voltage healthy conductors can assume relative to ground, and whether continued operation is permitted. Insulation monitoring becomes essential in systems intended to tolerate a first fault. The converter also limits and shapes fault current differently from a passive source. Downstream protection must coordinate with the recommended one-hundred-fifty-percent current window, the one-hundred-fifty-millisecond duration, DC-link energy, cable impedance, and device clearing time. Selectivity is a system result. It cannot be inferred from breaker ratings alone.

A floating system may continue after a first ground fault, but the healthy pole can shift in voltage relative to earth and a second fault can create a high-current path. High-resistance grounding establishes a controlled reference and detectable current, but resistor sizing and monitoring thresholds must fit the distribution. Insulation monitoring should distinguish transient behavior from a persistent fault and provide location support before maintenance begins. DC arcs do not pass through natural current zero every half-cycle as AC arcs do, so device voltage rating, polarity, interruption method, and enclosure design matter. Protection studies should use converter and capacitance models validated by test data.
