# pronunciation: PAM4 -> "PAM four"; PAM6 -> "PAM six"; PAM8 -> "PAM eight"; BER -> "B E R"; SNR -> "S N R"; RIN -> "R I N"; IMDD -> "I M D D"
At a fixed data rate, higher-order modulation lowers the symbol rate. Copper likes that because lower Nyquist frequency reduces insertion loss. Optics sees a different set of penalties.

At four hundred gigabits per lane, the transmitter must provide enough modulation bandwidth. The paper argues that conventional silicon-photonic modulation approaches face a substantial bandwidth challenge, while indium phosphide and newer thin-film materials have demonstrated stronger four-hundred-gigabit PAM four capability.

Higher modulation order also narrows the distance between signal levels. The receiver needs more optical power to maintain the same B E R or S N R. As power rises, shot noise and R I N become more important. Interferometric intensity noise and return-loss sensitivity also penalize PAM six and PAM eight more severely.

That is why the paper identifies PAM four as the most tractable I M D D format for four hundred gigabits per lane, even though copper may prefer PAM six.

This conclusion is forward-looking. Materials, integration, and pre-F E C requirements are still evolving. The point is that electrical and optical roadmaps should not assume the same modulation choice will optimize both media.
