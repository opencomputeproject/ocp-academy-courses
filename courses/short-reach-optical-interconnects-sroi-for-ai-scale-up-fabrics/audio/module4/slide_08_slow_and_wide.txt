# pronunciation: NRZ -> "N R Z"; WDM -> "W D M"; OCI MSA -> "O C I M S A"; SerDes -> "sir-dez"
Slow-and-wide optics takes a different approach to the bandwidth problem.

Instead of pushing one optical lane to the highest possible symbol rate, the architecture uses many lower-rate N R Z lanes. Lower modulation bandwidth can reduce optical power and make more photonic technologies viable.

W D M then combines multiple wavelengths on one fiber. Aggregate bandwidth grows through parallel lanes and colors rather than one narrow-and-fast signal. The O C I M S A roadmap is an example of this N R Z-plus-W D M direction across pluggable, on-board, and co-packaged placements.

The tradeoff does not disappear. A reverse gearbox may be needed between the fast host-chip interface and the wide optical engine. More lanes increase electrical I O and packaging complexity.

Wavelength routing, lasers, multiplexers, and demultiplexers add their own controls. If lanes remain on separate fibers, connector and fiber-management pressure grows.

The SROI channel makes slow-and-wide optical architectures plausible because a fiber run of thirty meters or less is benign. At that distance, system architects can partition an eight-hundred-gigabit pipe across more, slower lanes.

That approach can improve power, manufacturability, reliability, or supplier diversity. The objective is not maximum lane speed. It is the strongest whole-system tradeoff.
