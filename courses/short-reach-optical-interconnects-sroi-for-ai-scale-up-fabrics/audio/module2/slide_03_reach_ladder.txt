# pronunciation: CPC -> "C P C"; NPC -> "N P C"; OSFP -> "O S F P"
The reach ladder begins before the external cable.

At the shortest tier, co-packaged copper places the cable connection at the package boundary. Near-package copper uses a short fly-over path to avoid much of the printed-circuit-board loss. Both approaches preserve more of the channel budget for the cable itself.

A conventional backplane path spends budget in the package, board traces, connectors, and the cable assembly. Careful fly-over routing can make a same-rack path near one and a half meters feasible at two hundred twelve point five gigabits per second.

Signal conditioning can extend that range. Repeaters on an O S F P-style paddle card turn a passive assembly into active copper. Retimers placed around a backplane may support roughly two and a half to three meters, but they bring power and cooling with them.

Adjacent-rack routes often require three to seven meters after the actual service path is counted. That is where the ladder crosses into optics.

The lesson is that “reach” belongs to the topology. Package escape, board loss, connector choice, route geometry, and conditioning determine how much distance remains for the cable.
