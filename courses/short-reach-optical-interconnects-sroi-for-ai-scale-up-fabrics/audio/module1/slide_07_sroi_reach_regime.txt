# pronunciation: SROI -> "S R O I"; XSR -> "X S R"; VSR -> "V S R"; DR -> "D R"; FR -> "F R"; PCIe -> "P C I E"; CXL -> "C X L"; NVMe -> "N V M E"
Reach classes exist because different distances create different dominant impairments.

Electrical X S R and V S R links cross millimeters or centimeters with minimal equalization. Longer electrical channels add loss and signal conditioning. Conventional optical D R and F R specifications target hundreds of meters or kilometers, where attenuation, dispersion, and optical signal-to-noise ratio shape the design.

S R O I occupies a different region. The paper defines it up to thirty meters for intra-rack and inter-rack connectivity inside an AI Pod. Over that distance, single-mode fiber loss is small and chromatic dispersion is negligible. Multimode fiber also avoids a meaningful modal penalty at the lane rates considered.

That moves the optimization target to the endpoints. Designers care about optical lane density, energy per bit, cost per lane, latency, and reliability across very large link populations.

The physical medium is also protocol-agnostic. UALink, Ethernet scale-up networking, P C I E, C X L, and N V M E-related fabrics have different transaction semantics, but all approach copper’s physical limit. A common reach-defined optical layer can aggregate volume across those families instead of fragmenting the market into incompatible optical implementations.
