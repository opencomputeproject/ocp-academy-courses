Milliseconds, microseconds, and nanoseconds describe very different timescales. One millisecond is a thousand microseconds. One microsecond is a thousand nanoseconds. Those differences can change which communication opportunities are practical, but they do not create universal workload categories.

The paper uses fast switching to emphasize sub-millisecond operation, typically described as roughly ten to a thousand microseconds. The upper end of that approximate range reaches one millisecond. Treat it as a working description, not an exact certification boundary. The paper also explores still faster nanosecond research.

Millisecond-class systems can be useful when connectivity changes between jobs, during recovery, or on a longer planning interval. They may provide major value even when the topology remains fixed while the job runs. There is no requirement to use the fastest available mechanism for a change that happens infrequently.

Microsecond-class reconfiguration opens opportunities inside shorter communication cycles. The word opens is important: control, receivers, and endpoints must support the same timing before the opportunity becomes a usable system capability. Nanosecond demonstrations push toward even finer optical scheduling, often with custom transceivers and tightly coordinated architectures.

Keep three measurements separate. Device switching time describes the optical element or device boundary. End-to-end reconfiguration describes when the new connection is usable. Application performance describes the effect on useful work. A smaller number in the first category does not, by itself, establish an improvement in the other two.
