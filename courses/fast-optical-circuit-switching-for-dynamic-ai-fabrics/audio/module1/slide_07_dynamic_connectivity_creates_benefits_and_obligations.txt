Dynamic connectivity can move bandwidth toward the endpoints that need it. If demand shifts from one group of racks to another, reassigning optical capacity may reduce stranded bandwidth and improve the useful share of the fabric. A workload-aware topology can also reduce contention for selected communication phases.

Those benefits come with operating requirements. A changing topology needs coordinated scheduling, reliable state information, and a way to handle traffic while paths change. More rapid changes can increase the need for receiver reacquisition and can expose forwarding or transport assumptions that were harmless when links stayed stable for months.

Switching frequency also affects the lifetime question. A device intended to change state occasionally may face a very different duty cycle when asked to switch thousands of times per second. The relevant question is not simply whether it can move quickly once, but whether it can repeat the required operation reliably over its service life.

Energy and infrastructure savings should be evaluated at the system level. Avoided conversion and better utilization can help, while additional control, transceiver, and buffering requirements also consume resources. The net result depends on the actual design and workload.

Treat each proposed benefit as a hypothesis with a corresponding requirement. Better utilization needs demand that can use the reassigned capacity. Faster adaptation needs a coordinated end-to-end response. Lower cost or energy needs a fair comparison of the complete alternatives.
