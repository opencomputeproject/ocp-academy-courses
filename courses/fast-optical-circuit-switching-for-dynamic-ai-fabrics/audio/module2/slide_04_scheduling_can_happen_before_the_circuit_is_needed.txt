Scheduling answers which endpoints should connect next and when. In a demand-aware design, the controller needs a view of traffic demand, a way to choose useful connections, and a plan to coordinate the change. Gathering that view and computing the decision can take longer than changing the optical element.

One approach is to prepare the next schedule while the current one carries data. Fixed intervals, often called epochs, provide a convenient structure: use the present interval for communication and preparation, then activate the prepared state at the agreed boundary. A predictable workload can offer advance information about the next communication phase.

Another approach uses a precomputed sequence that does not depend on collecting current demand. It avoids part of the decision loop, but endpoints and routing must accommodate the sequence. A scheduled opportunity may require traffic to wait until the right connection appears. The absence of an online optimizer does not mean the system has no coordination cost.

Scaling from one switch to several introduces further dependencies. An end-to-end path may pass through multiple chassis. Each part must be available and configured consistently. If setup fails partway through, the requester needs a defined outcome rather than an assumption that every segment succeeded.

The engineering question is therefore broader than solver speed. Can the system prepare a valid path, coordinate all of its components, and report success or failure within the workload's budget? Precomputation helps when its assumptions remain valid; changing demand, failures, and calibration drift still need to be handled.
