Nanosecond research shows what coordinated optical systems can achieve under specific conditions. The most useful lesson is often the co-design, not simply the small number.

Sirius combines rapidly tunable transmitters with passive components that route light according to wavelength. Its research prototype demonstrated three point eight four nanoseconds of end-to-end reconfiguration on fifty-gigabit-per-second channels. That result relied on a specialized transmitter and closely coordinated timing and receiver techniques. It is not the performance of a conventional optical switch dropped into an otherwise unchanged network.

A separate Eindhoven demonstration reported forty-three point four nanoseconds for switching and control, and three point one nanoseconds for clock and data recovery. Those are separately described measurement boundaries. Do not substitute either number for application latency or casually combine them into a benchmark without checking the experimental sequence.

These studies address parts of the same systems problem: control must keep pace, receivers must recover, and contention and synchronization need explicit mechanisms. The remaining deployment questions include scale, interoperability, operational manageability, and integration with the intended networking stack.

Read each number with four labels: what was measured, where it was measured, what equipment and assumptions made it possible, and whether the result was experimental or simulated. This preserves the significance of the work while keeping the claim within the evidence.
