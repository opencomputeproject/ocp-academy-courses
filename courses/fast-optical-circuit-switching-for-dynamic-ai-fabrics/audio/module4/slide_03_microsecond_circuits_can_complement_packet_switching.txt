A hybrid network gives packet switching and optical circuits different responsibilities. The packet layer provides flexible connectivity, while scheduled circuits can carry traffic that benefits from a direct optical path. The opportunity is to allocate optical capacity where it is useful without assuming every transfer fits a circuit schedule.

Mordia is an early research example of microsecond optical reconfiguration. The prototype work reported approximately eleven point five microseconds of reconfiguration and explored a hybrid design. Traffic Matrix Scheduling coordinated circuit assignments in advance so communicating endpoints could use their allocated intervals efficiently.

The scheduling interval has consequences. Every transition consumes time during which the intended payload may not be transferred. Longer useful transmission intervals can amortize that cost, while shorter intervals can follow changing demand more closely. Buffering and schedule coordination determine how traffic waits between opportunities.

This explains why a switch result should be studied together with its control method. Faster reconfiguration can improve the useful fraction of a scheduled interval, but a poor schedule can still allocate capacity to endpoints that do not need it. Demand collection and prediction also have limits when traffic changes unexpectedly.

Use Mordia as evidence that microsecond circuit scheduling can be built and studied, not as a universal production performance promise. For a new workload, measure circuit utilization, queueing, delivered traffic, and the end-to-end application result. Keep the packet path and its operating cost in the comparison when it is part of the design.
