In single-phase immersion, the equipment sits in a qualified dielectric liquid. Heat passes from the immersed components into that liquid. The bath remains liquid throughout the intended cooling process, while its temperature changes as it receives and releases energy.

The system needs a way to transport the heat to an exchanger. Some arrangements use forced circulation. Others use natural convection within part of the cooling path. The important point is the complete designed circulation and heat-exchange arrangement, rather than an assumption that every tank operates in the same way.

At the exchanger, another cooling path receives the heat. The immersion fluid can then return at conditions suitable for the equipment. The external path still needs enough capacity to support the installation.

Follow the warm and cool paths in the animation. Keeping the fluid in one phase distinguishes this process from the boiling and condensation we'll examine next. It doesn't establish that the installation has low maintenance requirements, that a particular pump arrangement is sufficient, or that every component is compatible with the bath. Those are separate design and qualification questions. The phase label tells you how the fluid behaves, not whether the whole system is ready to deploy.
