Air and liquid often share the cooling work. A cold plate can collect heat from a processor while other components still depend on airflow. That remaining air-cooled load needs a path through the rack and the facility.

A rear-door heat exchanger addresses a different point in the heat path. Air leaving the equipment passes through an exchanger at the rack door, where heat transfers into liquid. The equipment may still use air to move heat away from its components. The door collects that heat farther downstream.

These arrangements can complement each other. The right combination depends on which components and loads each method covers. A highly liquid-cooled tray doesn't establish that the complete rack has no air-cooling requirement. The rack may contain power equipment, networking or other hardware with different thermal needs.

Be precise about the boundary behind a heat-capture claim. A percentage measured at one tray isn't automatically the percentage for a rack, a room or a building. Ask which heat is included, which heat remains, and how both paths are supported. A complete cooling design accounts for the whole installation.
