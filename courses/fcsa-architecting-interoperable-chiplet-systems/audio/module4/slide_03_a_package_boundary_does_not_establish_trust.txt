# pronunciation: F C S A; input output; nonvolatile memory; chiplet root of trust for attestation.

A package can contain chiplets from different suppliers and lifecycle histories. Physical enclosure does not establish identity, acceptable firmware, authorized debug state, or the intended topology. Those properties need evidence and policy. The security analysis begins with the assets, actors, and access paths that matter to the product.

F C S A distinguishes intra-chiplet domains from inter-chiplet domains. A bound inter-chiplet domain is composed through controlled trust relationships; attachable relationships have a different boundary and assumptions. Do not infer the security domain solely from a package outline. The question is which components and paths participate in a protected relationship and how that relationship is established and maintained.

For the accelerator case, identify valuable firmware, keys, system memory, configuration, and the evidence used to decide trust. Then consider who could alter the chiplet or reach debug and test mechanisms during manufacturing, integration, service, or operation. A controlled supply chain can affect the assessment only when product evidence supports the assumption and shows how it is maintained.

The chapter's SoC-equivalent security objective guides analysis across disaggregation. It does not supply definitive requirements for every product or replace a relevant assurance framework. Use the model to ask what security property could be lost at a newly exposed boundary, then identify the mechanisms and evidence needed to preserve it.
