Welcome back. In this video, we'll look at who owns and who manages data centers. As you'll see, the answer depends on the business model, the scale, and the purpose of the data center. Let's start with ownership. As of 2024, about 60% of the global IT workload is hosted in hyperscale data centers, those owned or leased by companies like Amazon, Google, Microsoft. These providers either build their own facilities or lease space in massive campuses. Hyperscale facilities are large, efficient, and designed for scale. When hyperscale companies own the data center, they're responsible for everything, from construction to energy efficiency to managing IT operations. When they lease, they manage the IT equipment and typically have stringent requirements for complete floors in the data center. But ultimately, they rely on a colocation partner to manage the physical facility. Now, let's talk about non-hyperscale environments. These include enterprise-owned data centers and colocation facilities. Together, they account for about 40% of the market. An enterprise-owned data center is built and operated by a single organization, usually to support internal IT needs. Think of a bank, a government agency, or an enterprise running its own data center for compliance and control. In a colocation model, multiple companies rent space, power, and cooling from a specialized third-party data center provider, but they install and manage their own servers and IT equipment. This model offers flexibility and reduces the need for capital investment. So who manages what? Let's break it down. Management in a data center is divided into two core functions: IT infrastructure management and facility infrastructure management. Building management is about keeping the lights on, literally. This includes power distribution, HVAC systems, physical security, and cooling. It's often managed through a BMS, or building management system. IT management, on the other hand, focuses on the servers, storage, and networking systems. These are tracked and managed using a DCIM, or a data center infrastructure management system. DCIM tools provide for visibility into how IT assets are performing and consuming resources. These two separate systems, BMS and DCIM, work together. One manages the environment, the other manages the workload. When integrated, they allow operators to respond proactively to issues and optimize performance. But data center management goes beyond just facilities and IT. A modern data center also operates as a business unit with responsibilities that include customer onboarding, contract management, sustainability, and compliance with global standards. Some of these standards are ISO 27000 for data security, ISO 14001 for environmental management, ISO 50001 for energy use, and new regulations like the EU CSRD directive. Together, these frameworks ensure that the data center is efficient, secure, sustainable, and accountable. Depending on the ownership model, either a third-party provider, an internal enterprise team, or a cloud provider manages these management layers. For example, in a colocation model, the BMS is managed by the provider, but the DCIM might be the tenant's responsibility. In summary, the key takeaway is this: ownership and management are not always the same. You can own and manage everything, lease and manage the IT only, or outsource both. Understanding these roles is key to navigating today's complex data center landscape.

Narration:
A facility operator, equipment owner and service operator can be different organizations. In colocation, the provider commonly supplies contracted space, power and supporting infrastructure while the customer operates its equipment. A cloud service can shift more of the stack to the provider.

The exact responsibilities depend on the contract and service model. Customers still need to understand their obligations for areas such as identities, data and configuration. An ownership label alone isn't a complete responsibility map.

When you think of data centers, you might be imagining rows of powerful buzzing machines. But what's actually running inside those machines? And how do organizations make the most of their hardware? That's where understanding physical versus virtual infrastructure becomes essential. Let's start with physical or bare metal infrastructure. In this setup, each piece of hardware runs one operating system, which in turn runs one application. This is known as a one-to-one model. It's simple and direct, but not always efficient. In many traditional deployments, you might have powerful servers running at only fifteen or thirty percent of their capacity. That's a lot of unused compute power, especially when hardware, power, and cooling costs add up. In fact, low utilization can be so persistent that some servers become what the industry calls zombie servers. These are machines that are still powered on using electricity and cooling, but no longer perform any meaningful work. Over time, they lose their original function, but stay online, draining resources without adding any value. To improve efficiency, organizations often move to virtualization. This is where one single physical server can host multiple virtual machines, each with its own operating system and application. It's like turning one powerful machine into many smaller ones. Virtualization is made possible by a software layer called a hypervisor. It divides the hardware resources like processing, memory, and storage amongst multiple virtual environments. Each virtual machine runs independently, even though they share the same physical server. This significantly boosts utilization, often above sixty percent or more. That means less hardware doing more work. But that's not the only evolution. The next step is containerization. Unlike virtual machines, containers share the host operating system that makes them lighter, faster, and easier to scale. Think of containers as highly efficient compartments, each running a single app. Containers are ideal for cloud-native applications, microservers, and workloads that need rapid scaling. They use fewer resources than traditional virtual machines and are especially useful in DevOps and modern CI/CD pipelines. Another way to look at this evolution is through utilization and redundancy. In traditional infrastructure, redundancy often means duplicating entire servers. It's expensive and inefficient. Virtualization, and especially containerization, allows for smarter redundancy with less hardware. So how do you choose between physical and virtual infrastructure? It mostly comes down to whether you need dedicated or shared resources. For highly specialized, highly performant tasks like scientific computing or real-time finance data, dedicated hardware, also called bare metal, gives you maximum power and control. But for most other workloads, shared infrastructure like virtual machines or containers offers greater speed, flexibility, and better cost savings. In summary, physical infrastructure gives you dedicated hardware and performance. Virtual machines add flexibility by sharing resources. Containers go even further by minimizing overhead and maximizing portability. Understanding this spectrum, physical, virtual, and containerized, is key to designing a modern data center. It's not about one being better than the other, but about choosing the right tool for the job.
