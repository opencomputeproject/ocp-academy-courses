Start with the datum, the shared reference used to locate features. Near and far describe positions relative to that datum. They don't mean front and rear in every possible server arrangement.

Now shorten the board at its far edge. Fixed features retain their positions relative to the datum. A fixed riser location or retention feature doesn't slide inward merely because the board becomes shorter. If a fixed feature ends up outside a smaller outline, that feature can fall outside the applicable board area. Read the specific requirements before deciding what remains necessary.

Floating features follow the relevant board edge. The far power zone and far-side connector or mounting references move with that edge, preserving their relationship to the boundary. The animation separates this edge-based movement from the datum-based locations that stay still.

This is a conceptual view of the reference rules, not a toleranced board drawing. It explains why a shorter board can reuse important interfaces without making every physical feature identical. A compatible tray may extend beyond the shorter board to reach the chassis's existing interlocks. Cables or other system details may also change.

The design review must preserve the right reference frame for each feature. Ask what stays anchored, what follows an edge, and which requirements still apply even when a particular chassis doesn't use that feature. That last question matters for far-edge keep out zones.
