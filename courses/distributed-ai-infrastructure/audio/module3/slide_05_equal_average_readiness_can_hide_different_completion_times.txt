A message leaves host A at a local reading of one hundred milliseconds. Host B records its arrival at ninety-seven. Has the message arrived before it was sent?

In this illustrative example, a shared reference shows the arrival at one hundred and three milliseconds. B's clock is six milliseconds behind that reference. Sorting the local timestamps alone reverses the apparent sequence.

The matching message identity connects the send to its receive. That dependency establishes causal order. Unrelated events don't acquire a causal relationship simply because one local clock shows an earlier time. Timestamps remain useful when their offset and uncertainty are understood.

Now separate that ordering question from performance. Consider one operation requiring four participants, with no overlapping work.

In the first example, all four contributions are ready at ten milliseconds. In the second, three are ready at five milliseconds and one at twenty-five. Both sets average ten milliseconds. Yet the group is ready at ten in the first example and twenty-five in the second.

The second group takes fifteen milliseconds longer to become ready. Its three early participants each wait twenty milliseconds: sixty participant-milliseconds altogether. That aggregate waiting time is different from elapsed delay.

These four samples demonstrate a dependency, not a diagnosis or a reliable percentile estimate. Investigate the affected steps using participant readiness, network and compute evidence, and correlated operation identities.
