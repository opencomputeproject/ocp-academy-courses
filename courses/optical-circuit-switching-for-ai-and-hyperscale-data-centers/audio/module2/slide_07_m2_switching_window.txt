A switching time becomes meaningful only when we compare it with the workload. Consider a cluster whose connections stay useful for a long communication phase. A relatively slow reconfiguration may be acceptable when the system can establish the circuits before that phase begins.

Now consider a different workload that needs new connections much more frequently. If reconfiguration consumes most of the available interval, the useful transfer window shrinks. The same switching mechanism can therefore be a good fit for one workload and a poor fit for another.

Robotic patching, mirror steering, polarization control, material deformation, on-chip routing, and phase steering provide different design options. Their actual timing depends on the implementation. Even within silicon photonics, electro-optic and thermo-optic actuation have different speed and power characteristics.

Do not stop at the fastest number. Check the required port count, the end-to-end optical loss budget, the power used to change and maintain a state, and the maturity of the design. The best physical fit is the one that satisfies these constraints within the workload’s available switching window.
