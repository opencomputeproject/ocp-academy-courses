ACTINA and OCS-BCube explore how optical connectivity can adapt to training workloads. Keep three things separate as you read this conceptual illustration: the physical connections that are available, the traffic that the workload needs to send, and the control decisions that allocate optical capacity. This is a research architecture, not a claim of a deployed production system.

During one iteration, some communicating groups need more bandwidth than others. A traffic-aware controller uses that demand to choose an allocation supported by the architecture. In the source concept, wavelength-reconfigurable transceivers are part of the mechanism that changes this allocation.

At the next iteration boundary, the demand changes. The controller revises the wavelength assignments and connectivity allocation. The physical topology still constrains which paths are possible; changing a wavelength does not create an arbitrary new fiber connection.

The following communication phase uses the revised allocation. The teaching point is the relationship between demand, control, and available optical paths. Performance depends on prediction, reconfiguration overhead, the transceiver design, and the workload. Research results must retain their evaluation conditions rather than become universal production promises.
