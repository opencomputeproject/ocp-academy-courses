Start with four endpoints and one optical circuit switch. The controller selects a connection from endpoint A to endpoint B. Inside the switch, the optical signal follows that established path. The switch does not need to read each packet to decide where the signal goes.

The electrical endpoints still generate and receive traffic. Packet inspection, buffering, and quality of service can exist in those surrounding systems. They are not functions supplied by the transparent optical path itself.

Now endpoint C also needs to communicate with B. The optical switch does not magically combine two independent demands onto the same dedicated connection. The broader system must decide how to allocate its available connectivity and when to change it.

This gives us two distinct activities: selecting a circuit, then transferring data through it. Bufferless operation removes packet queuing inside the optical switch. It does not promise that an entire network is free of contention. Keep that boundary in mind whenever you evaluate an OCS benefit.
